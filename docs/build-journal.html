What finished means for this run

  • Every item in the board above ends as merged, or as decided against with the measured reason written here.

16 Nov 2026Milestone 3pull request 35

A coach, and what the numbers say a coach is worth

The futsal idea

A futsal coach stands a metre from the court and changes how the team defends many times a match: press everywhere when a goal is needed, wait at halfway to protect a lead. Two things make a coach human. It does not react to every second of play, and the players do not hear it at once.

What was built

  • A Coach for a computer team. Every ten seconds of play it looks at the score and the clock; five seconds after it decides, the team plays what it asked for. Losing: push up. Winning in the last quarter: wait at halfway. Otherwise the team's usual tactic.
  • It reads only match snapshots and uses no randomness, so a seed still replays the same match. A scenario plays one seed twice to hold that.
  • --opponent-coach in the game (it replaces --opponent-follows-score) and home-coach / away-coach in the match runner, which now prints how often a coach changed tactic.

Decisions

  • Off by default. A coached opponent pushes up whenever it trails, and pushing up is the stronger way to defend. That sits close to the "push up for everyone" default the owner declined. The difficulty choice in the next milestone is where it gets turned on.
  • Never for the person's team. Their tactic is theirs, from the pause menu.
  • The look and the delay stay, but difficulty will not be built on them. Paired seed for seed against the same rule applied instantly, the delayed coach was better in 55 matches and worse in 52 (identical squads), 45 against 39 (rolled). That is noise. A "smarter coach" that only thinks faster would be a difficulty level nobody could feel. Difficulty has to come from what the numbers do separate: which tactic a team plays, and how good its players are.
  • A fix for two waiting teams: tried once, removed. When both teams wait at halfway nobody attacks: 0.9 goals and 9.5 shots a match. I gave the coach a rule to go and get a ball the other team keeps in its own half. It lifted the match to 1.2 goals (1.4 with patience cut from 20 s to 6 s). Not enough to keep the code. The state is also out of reach in a real game: a coach only waits when its team is ahead, and both teams cannot be ahead; and a person's own player always presses. It needs both teams to be told to wait by a launch option.

  • Coaches make draws rarer (74 to 60, 64 to 45): the team that falls behind changes something. Goals drop a little, and with identical squads 3.1 is close to the floor of 3.

Not exercised

  • Whether a person can tell that the opponent changed tactic, or feels it as fair.
  • --opponent-coach in the running game. It builds and the same coach runs in the match runner; nobody has played against it.
  • A coach that reads the play (who has the ball where, shots conceded) rather than the scoreboard. That is still not built.
16 Nov 2026Toolingpull request 34

Frames of states that only input can reach

The idea

Nobody is at the screen during this run, so the only way to look at a menu is to have the game write a drawn frame to a picture. Until now that picture was always the match two seconds in. The start screen, the difficulty choice and the way out of a match all sit behind a key press or a touch, so before building them the game needed a way to be pressed.

What was built

  • --script=0.5-0.6:P,1-1.1:0.5x0.62 plays back keys and touches at given times as if a person made them. A touch is written as fractions of the screen, so the same script works at any window size.
  • Two touches that end in the same #number are one finger, which makes a drag, and so the touch stick.
  • --capture-after=3 chooses when the picture is taken.

Decisions

  • Feed the script in where the keyboard and mouse are read, not further in. Everything after that point, including the menu's own handling of touches, runs exactly as it does for a person. A script that called the menu directly would have tested less.
  • Host only, with no scenario. It is a tool for looking, and the scenario runner cannot open a window. It was checked by three pictures: a key opened the pause menu, a touch chose a tactic in it, and a drag on the left half moved the player.

Not exercised

  • A gamepad cannot be scripted. Pad-only paths stay unseen.
  • A picture shows layout, never timing or feel.
16 Nov 2026Milestone 1pull request 33

Changing ends, a kickoff a person cannot fumble, and three rules left out

The futsal rule

Teams swap halves of the court at half-time, so neither keeps a better end for the whole match. On a court that is the same at both ends, swapping the teams is the same picture as walking round to the other side of the court and watching from there.

What was built

  • Changing ends as a turn of the view. The simulation is untouched: Home defends the west goal for the whole match. In the second half the picture is turned half a circle before it is drawn, and the stick is turned with it, so pushing up on the screen still moves the player up on the screen. Both turns live in the engine (AsSeen, FromScreen), where a scenario can check them.
  • A person's kickoff taker now stands behind the ball, facing forward. Since pull request 28 it stood on the far side facing its own half. Pushing the stick forward from there made it run away from the ball; the ball was knocked on at pass speed and reached the other team in a tenth of a second. A scenario reproduced this before the fix.
  • The first tap after a kickoff always finds a team-mate. By law everyone is in their own half, so every team-mate is behind the taker and a forward tap had nobody to find. For four seconds after a kickoff, a tap with nobody in front goes to the nearest team-mate.

Decisions

  • Turn the view, do not swap the teams. Swapping would mean every piece of code that knows which goal a team attacks has to ask what half it is. Turning the picture is one function and cannot unbalance anything: every measurement is identical with or without it.
  • On, with no setting. It is the law and football games have always done it. A setting to keep ends belongs with the settings screen if a person finds the turn disorienting.
  • The top bar keeps HOME on the left in both halves, as a television score bar does. Noted for the graphics rework.
  • The taker's side depends on who controls the team. I first faced every taker forward. Computers then carried the kickoff sideways and lost it: in the ten seconds after a goal the team that had just scored took 530 shots to the kicking team's 149, where the pass mark allows two to one. So a computer's taker keeps facing its own half, where it taps back at once. This is a shortcut and is marked as one in the code: with several people on a team it breaks, and the proper fix is to teach the computer a kickoff of its own.
  • Extra time: left out. There is no cup or league, so a draw is a result. Draws are common (74 of 300 with identical squads, 64 of 300 with rolled ones), so the day a knockout mode exists it will need this or penalties. Parked with the match flow.
  • Advantage: left out. Computers foul about once a match (1.2 with identical squads, 0.9 with rolled; most in one match: 6). Playing on after a foul could matter about once a match, and the one foul that exists takes the carrier off the ball from behind, which is the case where there is least to play on with.
  • Cards: left out for now, to be looked at again after a person plays. A second yellow needs the same player to foul twice. The runner does not record who fouled, but only 84 of 300 matches had two fouls at all, by anyone, so the true figure is well under that. A sending-off also needs a team that can play a player short for two minutes, which is a piece of computer play of its own. The caveat is real: these are computer numbers, and a person can slide far more often. For them the only deterrent today is the 10 m kick from the sixth foul.

Not exercised

  • Second-half controls by hand. A scenario shows that right on the screen moves the player right on the screen; nobody has felt it.
  • The moment of the turn: the camera and any effect still on screen when the half ends. Two still frames were looked at: a second-half kickoff seen from the other end (taken before the kickoff change), and a person's first-half kickoff with the taker behind the ball.
  • A person's kickoff, in either half, with a real stick.
  • Whether the turn confuses someone who was not expecting it.
15 Nov 2026Milestone 1pull request 32

The penalty, and accumulated fouls with the 10 m kick

The futsal rule

Futsal counts each team's fouls per half. The first five cost a free kick. From the sixth, every foul gives the other side a direct shot from the second penalty mark, 10 m out, with no defensive wall; if the foul was nearer the goal than that, the kick is taken from where it happened. The count starts again at half-time. This is the rule that makes a futsal team defend differently late in a half, and it is the one hardcore fans will look for first. A foul inside the area is a penalty from 6 m, as in football.

What was built

  • A foul count per team per half, shown beside each team's name in the top bar (F3 HOME 1 - 0 AWAY F5).
  • One mechanism for both kicks, since they differ only in the spot: the ball on the mark, the taker behind it facing the goal, every other outfielder of both teams behind the ball and 5 m from it until it is kicked.
  • The taker cannot move. The stick only aims; a tap passes and a held button shoots, as everywhere else.
  • The four-second count applies. A kick not taken becomes the defending team's free kick.

Decisions

  • Built although computers almost never reach it. Computer players decline a slide they can see is a foul, so they commit about one a match. In 300 matches with identical squads there was not one penalty or 10 m kick; with rolled squads no match had more than two. This rule exists for the person who slides at everything, and it cost little because the wall from the previous entry carried most of it.
  • The taker is frozen, unlike at a kick-in. Six metres from goal, letting the taker walk the ball forward would turn a penalty into a dribble at the goalkeeper.
  • The goalkeeper is not held on its line. At a 10 m kick the laws let it come out to 5 m; at a penalty it must stay on the line. The keeper's own positioning code runs unchanged. Marked as a shortcut.
  • No new controls. A penalty is aimed and struck with the same stick and button as any shot, which keeps the one-button promise.

Measured over 300 matches

  • Identical squads: every match identical to before, seed for seed. Rolled squads: goals 4.2 and shots 28.4 as before, results 109 / 65 / 126 against 110 / 64 / 126.
  • In scenarios a computer taker shoots both kinds of kick at the goal within the four seconds.
  • Five deliberate breakages (taker free to move, players not held behind the ball, no sixth-foul rule, no half-time reset, an untaken penalty handed over as a penalty) each made a scenario fail.

Not exercised

  • A person taking or conceding either kick. How often penalties are scored is unmeasured: there were too few to count.
  • The PENALTY and 10 M KICK banners, and the foul counts at two digits.
  • Whether a person notices the count at all before the sixth foul surprises them.
15 Nov 2026Milestone 1pull request 31

Five metres at a restart, and the four-second count

The futsal rule

At a kick-in, a corner or a free kick, opponents must stand at least 5 m from the ball until it is played. In return the taker has four seconds. Take longer and the ball goes to the other side: their kick-in, their free kick, or for a corner their goalkeeper's clearance. The two rules only make sense as a pair. Distance without a clock lets a team waste time; a clock without distance punishes a taker who was never given room.

What was built

  • The wall that already held opponents outside the centre circle at a kickoff became a general one: a spot, a radius, a team and a count. It blocks whoever moves the player, so a person and a computer are held alike, running or sliding.
  • The wall drops the moment the restart is played: the taker lets go of the ball, or carries it 3.5 m from the spot.
  • If the count reaches four seconds with the taker still there, play stops and the other team gets the restart. The banner reads FOUR SECONDS.
  • The match runner now counts four-second calls and goals scored soon after a restart.

Decisions

  • One wall, not four. The kickoff version was renamed and given a spot and radius instead of writing a second copy. Kickoff behaviour is unchanged.
  • "Played" is measured by distance, 3.5 m. A taker on a line stands a body inside it with the ball at its feet, which already puts the ball 2.3 m from the spot. My first value of 2 m switched the wall off before play began; the scenario caught it.
  • A taker may still dribble the ball in. The real rule says it must be kicked. Forcing a kick is a control change a person has to feel first, so it is left as a marked shortcut.
  • The kickoff keeps its softer rule. After four seconds the ball is open to a challenge and is not handed over. The laws have no four-second count for a kickoff.

  • Where the goals went. A probe of 40 matches with identical squads followed every restart. A match has about 60 kick-ins, and the taking team shoots within five seconds of about 25 of them. Those shots produced 3.2 goals a match before and 1.9 after, from slightly more shots. Corners fell the same way (22 goals to 8). The other team's goals were unchanged, so nobody was being robbed at a kick-in to begin with.
  • What the computer taker does. It carries the ball in at once and is 3.5 m from the spot a third of a second after play resumes, so between computers the wall stands for a third of a second. That, and defenders starting a body further back, is enough to make the first shot harder. Before, most goals in this game were a kick-in, three steps and a shot past defenders standing next to the taker.
  • Rule off, as a control. With the count set to zero only the starting distance changes (5 m to the edge of the body instead of to its centre). That alone took identical squads from 4.6 to 4.2 goals; the wall took them to 3.3.
  • A measurement I added and removed. "Goals within 10 s of a restart" said nothing, because play stops about 70 times in six minutes and nearly every goal falls inside that window. The probe with a five-second window, split by kind of restart, did the job.
  • Computer takers always play the ball in time. The count only ever stops a person who waits.
  • Identical squads at 3.3 goals sit near the floor of 3, and draws rose (56 to 76 in 300). Nothing planned lifts this: fouls are about one a match, so penalties and the 10 m kick will be rare. The lever, if a person finds matches too dry, is how computer defenders set up at a kick-in, not the pass marks.

Not exercised

  • A person taking or defending any restart. Whether being held at 5 m reads as a rule or as an invisible wall is a feel question.
  • The FOUR SECONDS banner was never drawn in a frame I looked at.
  • Which player a person controls after a four-second call goes against them.
  • Near a corner the edge of the court can leave a defender closer than 5 m.
15 Nov 2026Plan

How the rest of the work is ordered, and why

The idea

A long run with nobody watching fails in two ways: work stops at a question, or work drifts and nobody can tell what was decided. So the rules for both are fixed before the first line of code.

Decisions

  • Rules first, in dependency order. Earlier I skipped the four-second count and accumulated fouls because nothing kept opponents away from a restart: a taker who waits is robbed in under a second, so a count would only punish. The fix is to keep opponents 5 m away until the ball is played. That comes first; the count and the foul tally follow from it.
  • Scripted clicks before the screens. Three menus have shipped that I could draw but never click, because an agent has no hands. Before building a start screen, the frame-capture option learns to replay key and pointer events, so each new screen is driven end to end.
  • Platform for the touch build is chosen by what compiles. Whichever of Android or iOS builds on this machine and in CI goes first. It will be reported as never run on a device.
  • The kickoff tap question is closed in its own entry once measured, not left open.
  • Graphics are left alone. The run ends with a list of everything the renderer draws, as the starting point for the rework.

How every change is checked

  • Each new behaviour gets a scenario, and the scenario is shown to fail when the behaviour is broken on purpose.
  • Anything that changes the simulation is run over 300 seeded computer matches with rolled squads and 300 with identical squads, and compared seed by seed with the same matches before the change. 100 matches cannot tell two sides apart: identical teams once showed a win gap of 19.
  • The pass marks are goals 3–9 and shots 15–35 a match, at most 200 possessions, at least 3.5 m forward per possession; with identical squads also a win gap of at most 25 per 100 and no more than twice the shots for the team that just scored. A change that misses one has changed the game, so the cause is looked at before any mark moves.

What was already in place

The shape of the code

The match is a simulation that advances 60 times a second and contains no randomness in its physics. The same seed always replays the same match, which is what makes before-and-after comparison possible. Computer players send the same stick-and-button input a person does; they have no private way to move or tackle. The game window only reads input, steps the simulation and draws.
