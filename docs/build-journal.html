SWOF Build Journal

A running record of what gets built on the way to a releasable game, written so that each entry teaches the futsal rule or design idea behind it, the decision taken, what the numbers said, and what nobody has tried by hand yet.

Where each milestone stands

1. A real futsal matchclosed for this run

Kickoffs, halves with a change of ends, one foul, free kicks, 5 m at restarts, the four-second count, penalties and accumulated fouls with the 10 m kick exist. Cards, advantage and extra time are decided against for now, with the numbers in the entry below; cards are to be looked at again after a person has played.

2. Touchbuilds, never run on a device

The game reads real fingers and CI packages it as an Android app on every change. Nobody has installed that package on a phone. What a phone will show first is in the entry below.

3. Tactics as dataclosed for this run

Three defensive tactics, chosen from the pause menu, and a coach for a computer team that changes its tactic by the score. The coach is off until the difficulty choice turns it on. A fix for two waiting teams was tried once and decided against.

4. Product shellclosed for this run

A start screen, three difficulty levels, a pause menu with a way out, and a result at full time. The whole flow lives in the engine with scenarios, so new graphics only replace the drawing. No settings, no team choice, nothing saved between launches.

5. Release preparationpackaged builds from CI

The build machines can produce four downloads on request: Windows, macOS, Linux and Android. They are stopped by the account's billing at the moment, so none exists yet for the final commit. Signing, store listings and a public release are the owner's and were not touched.

What finished means for this run

  • Every item in the board above ends as merged, or as decided against with the measured reason written here.
  • Code decisions are taken without asking. The product name, the business model, legal review, store accounts and signing stay with the owner; the code is built up to them and no further.
  • Nothing here is a claim about how the game feels. A person has not played any of it. Each entry lists what was never exercised.
  • The look of the game is reworked after this run, so no effort goes into visuals now, and new screens keep their logic apart from their drawing.

Players at half the size, and the same game underneath

The idea

You said play felt crowded and the players looked wrong against the court. They were: a body was 1.05 m in radius on a 40 by 20 m court, about four times as wide as a person, and the ball was 0.42 m against a real 0.10 m. You looked at three sizes and chose the middle one: a body of 0.53 m and a ball of 0.21 m, still chunky in the Sensible Soccer way, with room between the players.

The interesting part is what a body's size was doing inside the game. Every rule about reaching the ball was written as "the two bodies touching, plus a bit": collecting a loose ball, keeping it while dribbling, the standing contest, the slide. Halve the bodies and every one of those reaches shrinks by 0.73 m without anyone deciding it should.

What the numbers said

  • Halving alone broke dribbling. Replaying one kickoff tick by tick showed the ball running loose from the carrier every second or so with no tackle anywhere near: the zone in which a dribbler keeps the ball had shrunk with the body.
  • Giving the reach back restored everything except goals. Same shots, fewer goals. The cause was the goalkeeper: its smaller body let it back up half a metre nearer its own line, and from there it saved more.
  • The kickoff drifted until opponents were held at the circle by their reach instead of their body; after that the two teams shoot equally often after a goal.

With mixed squads the same comparison over 300 matches gives 3.9 goals against 4.2 and 148 possessions against 147. All pass marks are met for both.

Decisions

  • Body and reach are now two numbers. The body is what you see and what stops at the boards. A new number, foot reach, is how far past the body a player's feet get to the ball, and it is set to exactly what the old large body used to supply. So the game plays as it did, measured, while looking half the size.
  • Why not shrink the reach too. I tried it: at two thirds of the old reach play still held up, at one third the ball changed hands 170 times a match and goals fell from 2.3 to 1.6 (both measured before the goalkeeper fix). More to the point, a shorter reach asks a person to steer closer and time later, and the computer playing itself cannot feel that. It is one number to turn down once you have played it.
  • The camera shows 20 m of the court's length, not 28. That keeps a figure about as readable as before. Speeds are unchanged in metres, so the same run now crosses the screen about 1.4 times faster.
  • Opponents at a kickoff or a restart stand a little further back than their bodies alone would need: far enough that a stretched leg is outside the distance.

Not exercised

  • Nobody has played it. Whether a player now seems to pick the ball up from too far away, whether tackles look fair, and whether the faster-looking pace is welcome are all yours to judge.
  • The old player pictures are simply drawn smaller, about three and a half times their size instead of five, so their pixels are uneven. New pictures are the third job of the five.
  • The court picture is stretched a little more than before and its lines are slightly softer.
  • Not tried on a phone, where a 113-pixel figure on a desktop window becomes much smaller.
16 Nov 2026Checkspull request 40

Checks that catch what a passing test can miss

The idea

A published account of how the Claude Code team checks its own work describes four kinds of check. Comparing a change with the code before it. Feeding in random input and checking rules that must always hold. Running a cheap gate before an agent may stop. Writing down what a reviewer looks for. The match engine is deterministic: the same seed plays the same match every time. That makes the first two cheap to build here.

What was added

  • A before-and-after comparison. tools/check.sh diff plays the same 100 matches on main and on the changed code, and fails on any difference. A tidy-up, a new look or a docs change must show none. A gameplay change shows which matches it moved.
  • Rules that always hold, checked every tick. The clock moves one tick. A score never falls and never jumps by two. The half never goes back. At most one player has the ball. No speed or position becomes a broken number. A new scenario breaks each rule on purpose to prove it is caught.
  • A pretend person mashing the controls. Ten of the hundred matches are replayed with Home's stick and button worked at random. Once, all hundred were played that way: every match reached full time with no rule broken.
  • A gate before an agent stops. When the code has changed, an agent working in this repository runs the fast checks before it ends its turn. If they fail, it is sent back to fix them. A version that already passed is not checked twice.
  • A review checklist, REVIEW.md, grouped by which part of the code a change touches.

Decisions

  • Every new check was shown to fail as well as pass. A check that only ever passes may not be checking anything. Changing one goalkeeper number failed the comparison and blocked the stop gate. Each always-hold rule was broken by hand.
  • A failing scenario is fixed, never retried or skipped. Nothing here is random, so a result that changes means the code changed. After three failed attempts at the same failure, the agent stops and reports what it tried.
  • Merged on local evidence. The build machines are still stopped by billing. The full local gate passed, and the comparison showed all 100 matches identical to main: the new checks did not change the game.

Not exercised

  • How the game feels. Random input finds crashes and broken rules, not dull or unfair play.
  • The stop gate inside a real agent session. It was fed sample input by hand; it loads only in a new session.
  • The new checks on Linux and Windows, and the Android build, because CI cannot run.

Everything the game draws, written down before anyone redraws it

The idea

The planned development work is finished, and the next piece is yours: the look. A rework goes faster when it starts from a list of what exists rather than from a blank page, because the list shows what is art, what is code pretending to be art, and what the game knows but never shows. The list is in the repository as docs/renderer-inventory.md. Nothing about the look was changed.

What the inventory found

  • Three image files are all the art. One font and two player sheets with frames of 24 by 32 pixels: one standing frame and four running frames for each team. On screen each frame is enlarged about five times.
  • Everything else is drawn by code from a white pixel, a disc and a ring: the court, the ball, shadows, rings, every effect, the scoreboard and the menus.
  • A player has two states. Standing and running. A kick, a slide, a save and a goal celebration have no pictures; a kick is the same picture squashed and nudged.
  • Teams are painted into the sheets. Home is red, Away is blue, goalkeepers are the same picture tinted. No two team-mates look different.
  • The game knows things nothing shows: the officials, each player's role, and a sentence for every stoppage.
  • One door. All drawing goes through one call from one place. A new renderer that accepts the same information can replace the old one without touching the match itself.

Decisions

  • Oddities were listed, not fixed. Nine were found, among them a banner that still names a key that no longer works, a developer's note shown to every player when the game opens, and a font whose right to be shipped as a file has not been established. Each would be a small fix today and a wasted one if the rework replaces the thing it sits in, so the rework settles them once.
  • The last two pull requests were merged on local evidence. GitHub stopped starting build jobs part-way through this entry: the account's Actions spending limit or payment needs the owner's attention. The code in pull request 38 had already passed every job before only its notes changed; pull request 39 adds documents only. The full local gate (every scenario, the game build, the format check) passed on the final commit. The build machines will start again once billing is sorted.
  • Packaging now runs only when asked. The repository is private, and a private repository pays for build minutes; a macOS machine costs ten times a Linux one, and two of the three desktop builds use it. Packaging on every change cost roughly 24 paid minutes each time, on top of about 26 for the ordinary checks, and this run pushed many times. A package is wanted when someone is about to hand the game out, not on every change, so it is now one command: gh workflow run Package --ref main.

Not exercised

  • The inventory was read from the source and from a few captured frames. Nobody compared each item with the running game by eye.
  • One oddity (light see-through colours coming out too bright) is a reading of the code, not something seen. A tenth, a supposed slip in how players react to an event, was struck after checking how the language reads that line: the code is right.
  • No packaged build exists for the final commit, on any system. The ones checked in the entry below were made from the same game code one commit earlier. After billing is fixed: gh workflow run Package --ref main for the desktop folders and gh workflow run CI --ref main, or any new push, for the checks and the Android package.
16 Nov 2026Milestone 5pull request 38

A folder you can hand to someone, built the same way every time

The idea

Until now the game ran only from its source code, on a machine with the .NET kit installed. A player has neither. A packaged build is the game as a player receives it: one folder holding the program, its three content files and the .NET runtime itself, so nothing has to be installed first. "Reproducible from CI" means no person builds it by hand: a build machine makes it from a named commit, the same way each time, and anyone can download the result.

What was built

  • A second workflow, Package. On demand it publishes the game three times, for Windows, macOS on Apple chips, and Linux, and stores each as a download named after the system and the commit.
  • The Android package already comes from the build added in the last entry. Together that is four downloads for any commit you ask for.
DownloadSizeChecked
Windows, 64-bit36 MBlisted: program and three content files present
macOS, Apple chips34 MBlisted, then started here: it drew the start screen

Decisions

  • The runtime travels inside the folder. It triples the size, and in exchange the player installs nothing. For a first hand-out to testers, "unpack and run" is worth 25 MB.
  • The Linux folder is made on a Mac build machine. Building the game's font file needs Arial, which Linux build machines do not have. Any system can publish for any other, so the Linux folder comes from the machine that has the font. The font itself is one of the things the graphics rework should replace with one the project is known to be free to ship.
  • Packed as a tar file. The download store's own zip forgets which file is the program, and on macOS and Linux a program that has lost that mark will not start. A tar file keeps it.
  • Stopped before anything in the owner's name. No signing, no notarizing, no store listing, no public release page. Each needs an account, an identity or a licence that only the owner can accept, and a name that is not final yet.

Not exercised

  • The Windows and Linux folders were never started, only listed.
  • The macOS folder is not signed or notarized and is not an app bundle. Fetched through a browser, macOS will refuse to open it until the owner allows it by hand. It started here because the command-line download does not carry that mark.
  • There is no Intel Mac build and no iPhone build.
  • No icon, version number or installer work was done beyond what was already there.
16 Nov 2026Milestone 2pull request 37

Real fingers, and an Android package nobody has installed yet

The idea

The controls were designed for a phone from the start: a stick under the left thumb, one button under the right. Until now a mouse played the part of one finger, which cannot hold the stick and press the button at once. Two things were missing: reading real fingers, and a build of the game that a phone can install.

What was built

  • Real touches. The game asks the screen for every finger that is down and hands each one, with the id the screen gave it, to the touch controls that already existed. Those controls and the menus were written against finger ids and already tested with two fingers, so nothing in the engine changed. The mouse stays as one more finger for trying things at a desk.
  • An Android launcher, Swof.Droid. It is one small file. Everything else is the same source as the desktop game, compiled against the Android version of the framework. On a phone the game asks for full screen and landscape.
  • A third CI job, build-android. Every pull request now compiles the game for Android, packages it and keeps the package as a download. The first green run produced an 11 MB package holding the game, the engine and all three content files, for 64-bit ARM phones and for emulators.

Decisions

  • Android first, because of what could be checked. This machine has no Xcode and no Android kit, so nothing mobile compiles here. An iPhone build needs a Mac build machine with Xcode and an Apple signing identity. An Android build needs only tools that CI machines already carry. I did not install an Android kit here: that means accepting licences in the owner's name, which is not a code decision.
  • One set of game sources, no copy. The Android project compiles the desktop project's files in place. The three spots where a phone must differ ask the operating system at run time. A second copy of the game would drift within a week.
  • The app's identity is a placeholder (com.example.swof). The real one belongs with the store account and the name, which are the owner's. The package is signed with a throwaway debug key, which is enough to install by hand and not enough for a store.
  • The name on the phone's home screen comes from the same single constant as the window title and the start screen.
  • The content tool's manifest moved to the repository root so both projects find it. That was the one failure on the way: the first Android run compiled the code and then could not find the tool that packs the font and the sprites.

Not exercised, and what to expect on a first install

  • The package has never been installed. "It builds" means the code compiles and the files are inside. It does not mean it starts.
  • Likely first findings on a phone, none of them handled: the game does not pause itself when the app goes to the background; nothing keeps clear of a notch or rounded corners; the scoreboard text was sized for a desktop window; the sound was only ever heard on a desktop.
  • Two thumbs at once have only been played by scripted touches. Missed taps, a thumb sliding off the stick, and the size of the pause button under a real finger are all unknown.
  • The touch reading itself runs on the desktop build too, where it reports no fingers on this machine. Scripted touches and the mouse still work there; that was checked with a captured frame.

A start screen, a way out, and three levels that were measured before they were named

The idea

A game is a product once a stranger can start it, lose, pick an easier opponent and try again without being told a key. Until now the game opened straight into a match and the only way out was to close the window. The other half of this entry is difficulty: what can make a computer team easier or harder without cheating, and how to find out before a person has played.

What was built

  • The match flow, in the engine. MatchFlow knows four screens: start, playing, paused, full time. It holds the heading, the menu items, the cursor, the chosen difficulty and the person's tactic. The game host draws what it is given and obeys three events: start a match, the match was left, the tactic changed. When the graphics are redone, none of this logic moves.
  • Start screen: the product's name, Play, and a difficulty that goes round normal, hard, easy. Pause: resume, the three tactics with the one in use marked, Leave match. Full time: the result, New match, Leave match.
  • One stick and one button work every menu, the same two controls as the match, so a pad or a keyboard needs nothing extra. A held stick moves the cursor one item; a held button chooses once. Touching an item chooses it directly.
  • The product's name is written once, in Product.Name. The folder where tuning is saved keeps its own literal on purpose: a rename must not lose a saved file.
  • Difficulty as data: Difficulty.Easy, Normal, Hard. The match runner can play a level (away-difficulty=hard), so the numbers below are for exactly what the game uses.

A bug the captured frames caught

The first press of the button on Play started the match and, a tenth of a second later, passed the kickoff away: letting go of the button was read by the match as the end of a tap. A finger did not do this, because a touch that lands on a menu stays the menu's until it lifts. The button now follows the same rule: a button that was down while a menu was open is not the match's until it has been let go. A scenario plays the kickoff through the input buffer to hold it.

16 Nov 2026Milestone 3pull request 35

A coach, and what the numbers say a coach is worth

The futsal idea

A futsal coach stands a metre from the court and changes how the team defends many times a match: press everywhere when a goal is needed, wait at halfway to protect a lead. Two things make a coach human. It does not react to every second of play, and the players do not hear it at once.

What was built

  • A Coach for a computer team. Every ten seconds of play it looks at the score and the clock; five seconds after it decides, the team plays what it asked for. Losing: push up. Winning in the last quarter: wait at halfway. Otherwise the team's usual tactic.
  • It reads only match snapshots and uses no randomness, so a seed still replays the same match. A scenario plays one seed twice to hold that.
  • --opponent-coach in the game (it replaces --opponent-follows-score) and home-coach / away-coach in the match runner, which now prints how often a coach changed tactic.

Decisions

  • Off by default. A coached opponent pushes up whenever it trails, and pushing up is the stronger way to defend. That sits close to the "push up for everyone" default the owner declined. The difficulty choice in the next milestone is where it gets turned on.
  • Never for the person's team. Their tactic is theirs, from the pause menu.
  • The look and the delay stay, but difficulty will not be built on them. Paired seed for seed against the same rule applied instantly, the delayed coach was better in 55 matches and worse in 52 (identical squads), 45 against 39 (rolled). That is noise. A "smarter coach" that only thinks faster would be a difficulty level nobody could feel. Difficulty has to come from what the numbers do separate: which tactic a team plays, and how good its players are.
  • A fix for two waiting teams: tried once, removed. When both teams wait at halfway nobody attacks: 0.9 goals and 9.5 shots a match. I gave the coach a rule to go and get a ball the other team keeps in its own half. It lifted the match to 1.2 goals (1.4 with patience cut from 20 s to 6 s). Not enough to keep the code. The state is also out of reach in a real game: a coach only waits when its team is ahead, and both teams cannot be ahead; and a person's own player always presses. It needs both teams to be told to wait by a launch option.

  • Whether a person can tell that the opponent changed tactic, or feels it as fair.
  • --opponent-coach in the running game. It builds and the same coach runs in the match runner; nobody has played against it.
  • A coach that reads the play (who has the ball where, shots conceded) rather than the scoreboard. That is still not built.

Frames of states that only input can reach

The idea

Nobody is at the screen during this run, so the only way to look at a menu is to have the game write a drawn frame to a picture. Until now that picture was always the match two seconds in. The start screen, the difficulty choice and the way out of a match all sit behind a key press or a touch, so before building them the game needed a way to be pressed.

  • Host only, with no scenario. It is a tool for looking, and the scenario runner cannot open a window. It was checked by three pictures: a key opened the pause menu, a touch chose a tactic in it, and a drag on the left half moved the player.

Not exercised

  • A gamepad cannot be scripted. Pad-only paths stay unseen.
  • A picture shows layout, never timing or feel.
16 Nov 2026Milestone 1pull request 33

Changing ends, a kickoff a person cannot fumble, and three rules left out

The futsal rule

Teams swap halves of the court at half-time, so neither keeps a better end for the whole match. On a court that is the same at both ends, swapping the teams is the same picture as walking round to the other side of the court and watching from there.

What was built

  • Changing ends as a turn of the view. The simulation is untouched: Home defends the west goal for the whole match. In the second half the picture is turned half a circle before it is drawn, and the stick is turned with it, so pushing up on the screen still moves the player up on the screen. Both turns live in the engine (AsSeen, FromScreen), where a scenario can check them.
  • A person's kickoff taker now stands behind the ball, facing forward. Since pull request 28 it stood on the far side facing its own half. Pushing the stick forward from there made it run away from the ball; the ball was knocked on at pass speed and reached the other team in a tenth of a second. A scenario reproduced this before the fix.
  • The first tap after a kickoff always finds a team-mate. By law everyone is in their own half, so every team-mate is behind the taker and a forward tap had nobody to find. For four seconds after a kickoff, a tap with nobody in front goes to the nearest team-mate.

Decisions

  • Turn the view, do not swap the teams. Swapping would mean every piece of code that knows which goal a team attacks has to ask what half it is. Turning the picture is one function and cannot unbalance anything: every measurement is identical with or without it.
  • On, with no setting. It is the law and football games have always done it. A setting to keep ends belongs with the settings screen if a person finds the turn disorienting.
  • The top bar keeps HOME on the left in both halves, as a television score bar does. Noted for the graphics rework.
  • The taker's side depends on who controls the team. I first faced every taker forward. Computers then carried the kickoff sideways and lost it: in the ten seconds after a goal the team that had just scored took 530 shots to the kicking team's 149, where the pass mark allows two to one. So a computer's taker keeps facing its own half, where it taps back at once. This is a shortcut and is marked as one in the code: with several people on a team it breaks, and the proper fix is to teach the computer a kickoff of its own.
  • Extra time: left out. There is no cup or league, so a draw is a result. Draws are common (74 of 300 with identical squads, 64 of 300 with rolled ones), so the day a knockout mode exists it will need this or penalties. Parked with the match flow.
  • Advantage: left out. Computers foul about once a match (1.2 with identical squads, 0.9 with rolled; most in one match: 6). Playing on after a foul could matter about once a match, and the one foul that exists takes the carrier off the ball from behind, which is the case where there is least to play on with.
  • Cards: left out for now, to be looked at again after a person plays. A second yellow needs the same player to foul twice. The runner does not record who fouled, but only 84 of 300 matches had two fouls at all, by anyone, so the true figure is well under that. A sending-off also needs a team that can play a player short for two minutes, which is a piece of computer play of its own. The caveat is real: these are computer numbers, and a person can slide far more often. For them the only deterrent today is the 10 m kick from the sixth foul.

The penalty, and accumulated fouls with the 10 m kick

The futsal rule

Futsal counts each team's fouls per half. The first five cost a free kick. From the sixth, every foul gives the other side a direct shot from the second penalty mark, 10 m out, with no defensive wall; if the foul was nearer the goal than that, the kick is taken from where it happened. The count starts again at half-time. This is the rule that makes a futsal team defend differently late in a half, and it is the one hardcore fans will look for first. A foul inside the area is a penalty from 6 m, as in football.

What was built

  • A foul count per team per half, shown beside each team's name in the top bar (F3 HOME 1 - 0 AWAY F5).
  • One mechanism for both kicks, since they differ only in the spot: the ball on the mark, the taker behind it facing the goal, every other outfielder of both teams behind the ball and 5 m from it until it is kicked.
  • The taker cannot move. The stick only aims; a tap passes and a held button shoots, as everywhere else.
  • The four-second count applies. A kick not taken becomes the defending team's free kick.

Decisions

  • Built although computers almost never reach it. Computer players decline a slide they can see is a foul, so they commit about one a match. In 300 matches with identical squads there was not one penalty or 10 m kick; with rolled squads no match had more than two. This rule exists for the person who slides at everything, and it cost little because the wall from the previous entry carried most of it.
  • The taker is frozen, unlike at a kick-in. Six metres from goal, letting the taker walk the ball forward would turn a penalty into a dribble at the goalkeeper.
  • The goalkeeper is not held on its line. At a 10 m kick the laws let it come out to 5 m; at a penalty it must stay on the line. The keeper's own positioning code runs unchanged. Marked as a shortcut.
  • No new controls. A penalty is aimed and struck with the same stick and button as any shot, which keeps the one-button promise.

Measured over 300 matches

  • Identical squads: every match identical to before, seed for seed. Rolled squads: goals 4.2 and shots 28.4 as before, results 109 / 65 / 126 against 110 / 64 / 126.
  • In scenarios a computer taker shoots both kinds of kick at the goal within the four seconds.
  • Five deliberate breakages (taker free to move, players not held behind the ball, no sixth-foul rule, no half-time reset, an untaken penalty handed over as a penalty) each made a scenario fail.

Not exercised

  • A person taking or conceding either kick. How often penalties are scored is unmeasured: there were too few to count.
  • The PENALTY and 10 M KICK banners, and the foul counts at two digits.
  • Whether a person notices the count at all before the sixth foul surprises them.

Five metres at a restart, and the four-second count

The futsal rule

At a kick-in, a corner or a free kick, opponents must stand at least 5 m from the ball until it is played. In return the taker has four seconds. Take longer and the ball goes to the other side: their kick-in, their free kick, or for a corner their goalkeeper's clearance. The two rules only make sense as a pair. Distance without a clock lets a team waste time; a clock without distance punishes a taker who was never given room.

What was built

  • The wall that already held opponents outside the centre circle at a kickoff became a general one: a spot, a radius, a team and a count. It blocks whoever moves the player, so a person and a computer are held alike, running or sliding.
  • The wall drops the moment the restart is played: the taker lets go of the ball, or carries it 3.5 m from the spot.
  • If the count reaches four seconds with the taker still there, play stops and the other team gets the restart. The banner reads FOUR SECONDS.
  • The match runner now counts four-second calls and goals scored soon after a restart.

Decisions

  • One wall, not four. The kickoff version was renamed and given a spot and radius instead of writing a second copy. Kickoff behaviour is unchanged.
  • "Played" is measured by distance, 3.5 m. A taker on a line stands a body inside it with the ball at its feet, which already puts the ball 2.3 m from the spot. My first value of 2 m switched the wall off before play began; the scenario caught it.
  • A taker may still dribble the ball in. The real rule says it must be kicked. Forcing a kick is a control change a person has to feel first, so it is left as a marked shortcut.
  • The kickoff keeps its softer rule. After four seconds the ball is open to a challenge and is not handed over. The laws have no four-second count for a kickoff.

How the rest of the work is ordered, and why

The idea

A long run with nobody watching fails in two ways: work stops at a question, or work drifts and nobody can tell what was decided. So the rules for both are fixed before the first line of code.

Decisions

  • Rules first, in dependency order. Earlier I skipped the four-second count and accumulated fouls because nothing kept opponents away from a restart: a taker who waits is robbed in under a second, so a count would only punish. The fix is to keep opponents 5 m away until the ball is played. That comes first; the count and the foul tally follow from it.
  • Scripted clicks before the screens. Three menus have shipped that I could draw but never click, because an agent has no hands. Before building a start screen, the frame-capture option learns to replay key and pointer events, so each new screen is driven end to end.
  • Platform for the touch build is chosen by what compiles. Whichever of Android or iOS builds on this machine and in CI goes first. It will be reported as never run on a device.
  • The kickoff tap question is closed in its own entry once measured, not left open.
  • Graphics are left alone. The run ends with a list of everything the renderer draws, as the starting point for the rework.

How every change is checked

  • Each new behaviour gets a scenario, and the scenario is shown to fail when the behaviour is broken on purpose.
  • Anything that changes the simulation is run over 300 seeded computer matches with rolled squads and 300 with identical squads, and compared seed by seed with the same matches before the change. 100 matches cannot tell two sides apart: identical teams once showed a win gap of 19.
  • The pass marks are goals 3–9 and shots 15–35 a match, at most 200 possessions, at least 3.5 m forward per possession; with identical squads also a win gap of at most 25 per 100 and no more than twice the shots for the team that just scored. A change that misses one has changed the game, so the cause is looked at before any mark moves.
